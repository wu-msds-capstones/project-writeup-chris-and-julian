Introduction

American workers anticipate that automation will transform their jobs. According to Gallup’s report, concerns about technology rendering jobs obsolete have significantly increased between 2021 and 2023 (Saad 2023). The Pew Research Center’s findings indicate that Americans anticipate automation to displace other workers while preserving their own jobs largely intact (Smith and Anderson 2017). However, this concern is not uniformly distributed across the country, as the most susceptible industries to automation are concentrated in specific regions. Across the counties analyzed in this study, which collectively house approximately 282 million people, or about 84 percent of the United States population (U.S. Census Bureau 2023), routine work constitutes approximately 37 percent of the measured task content. This paper explores the question of whether the composition of local work is associated with the affordability of living in the respective areas.

Prior research laid the groundwork for our current framework. Autor, Levy, and Murnane (2003) demonstrated that computers replace routine tasks and enhance non-routine tasks, enabling us to identify occupations susceptible to automation. Autor and Dorn (2013) applied this framework to local labor markets, revealing polarization in routine-intensive areas. In these areas, employment grew at both the high and low ends of the wage distribution, while middle-wage employment declined. Acemoglu and Autor (2011) generalized this approach into a model that explains how technology redistributes work across skill groups. This model remains the standard framework for empirical research on automation and labor markets.

These studies, while providing a robust framework for comprehending automation and labor market shifts, don’t directly address our inquiry about county-level affordability. Firstly, the outcomes are measured in unadjusted wages and employment shares, rather than local purchasing power. Consequently, a high-wage metropolitan area and a low-wage rural county can appear vastly different in dollar terms, even when the goods those wages can purchase are comparable. Secondly, the unit of analysis is the commuting zone, which averages over the variations among the counties it encompasses. Neither of these limitations is a flaw in the earlier studies, which were designed for different purposes, but they do restrict their ability to explain differences in affordability at the county level.

This project explores the relationship between the task composition of local work and county affordability, specifically examining whether this relationship persists after accounting for poverty and unemployment. We construct a county-by-year panel spanning from 2008 to 2023, excluding 2020, using datasets published by federal agencies. Each county year is categorized into four task groups: routine cognitive, routine manual, non-routine cognitive, and non-routine manual. These task groups align with the task framework of Autor, Levy, and Murnane (2003) and the occupation taxonomy of Autor and Dorn (2013). The primary outcome of this analysis is purchasing power, measured in dollars as the county median household income divided by the local price level obtained from the Bureau of Economic Analysis Regional Price Parities. The panel encompasses 848 counties and comprises 11,983 county-year observations.

We estimate a panel regression model with year indicators and county-level clustered standard errors. This approach allows us to recover a meaningful estimate of the association between variables while accounting for the correlation between repeated observations within the same county. To validate our model specification, we examine residual plots, quantile-quantile (QQ) plots, and variance inflation factors. These diagnostics reveal that purchasing power changes proportionally rather than in fixed dollar increments, and a log transformation effectively captures this structure. Consequently, we opt to fit random forest and neural network models, which can represent the portion of the relationship that a linear model cannot. The complexity of these models increases only when the diagnostics indicate that a simpler specification is insufficient.

The central estimate is that a one percentage point shift toward non routine manual work, away from non routine cognitive, is associated with roughly $1,041 less in purchasing power.

Background and Related Work

The project begins with a change in how economists think about what technology replaces. Autor, Levy, and Murnane (2003) argued that technology does not entirely replace occupations, rather, it replaces tasks. Tasks that follow explicit, rule based procedures can be documented as programs, making computers suitable substitutes. On the other hand, tasks that require judgment, adaptability, or situational response resist this translation and are instead complemented. By categorizing tasks along two axes, routine versus non-routine and cognitive versus manual, we can identify the four groups we use here. This transformation turns a vague concern about automation into a measurable concept.

Once task scores become measurable at the occupational level, they can also be measured at the place level. This is because places differ in the types of work their residents do. Autor and Dorn (2013) demonstrated this by showing that local labor markets have long-lasting differences in their composition of work, which contributed to the polarization of employment across US commuting zones. Routine intensive areas experienced a decline in middle-wage jobs and an increase in employment at both the low and high ends of the wage distribution. Their occupation taxonomy is the one we use when categorizing occupations into task groups. Their finding that task groups are a persistent characteristic of a place, rather than a temporary feature of its residents, makes a panel design a sensible approach.

Acemoglu and Autor (2011) laid the formal groundwork for this approach. Their model conceptualizes tasks as the production units, with skill groups vying to supply them. Technological shifts determine which group holds the comparative advantage in which task. Consequently, local outcomes are contingent upon the tasks performed within a local economy. This framework has become the standard for empirical research on automation and labor markets, and our county-level design incorporates its core assumption.

Alongside this research, a separate line of evidence focuses on what workers themselves anticipate. Gallup’s findings indicate a significant increase in concerns about technology potentially rendering jobs obsolete between 2021 and 2023 (Saad 2023). Similarly, Pew’s reports suggest that Americans anticipate automation to displace other workers while preserving their own jobs largely intact (Smith and Anderson 2017). These surveys measure beliefs rather than task scores, and it’s possible for these two measures to diverge. For instance, a worker in a county with a high concentration of routine work may not be as worried as one in a county with a lower concentration, and vice versa. This discrepancy between worker expectations and the actual composition of local work is a key motivation behind directly measuring the latter.

The purchasing power of a wage, measured in dollars, varies depending on the location where it is earned. Prior work reports outcomes in unadjusted wages and employment shares, so a high-wage metropolitan area and a low-wage rural county can appear vastly different in dollar terms even when the goods those wages can buy are comparable. Geography further complicates matters because the commuting zone averages over the counties it encompasses, obscuring the variation that determines whether a paycheck covers rent in any specific county. Additionally, estimation is typically linear, so whether purchasing power varies with task groups in fixed dollar increments or proportionally is not something these designs can address. None of these limitations are flaws in the earlier studies, which were intended to address different questions. However, together, these limitations restrict the extent to which we can say about county-level purchasing power.

Data

Data Collection

Data for this analysis come from five datasets published by three U.S. federal agencies. Each was retrieved through an agency API or bulk file download and loaded into a PostgreSQL database. Table 1 reports each source, record counts, and the variables it supplies.

The five sources arrive at two levels of observation: county by year, and metropolitan area. The pipeline transforms each to a common county by year grain, and county FIPS code and year together serve as the join key across the warehouse. Regional Price Parities require one extra step. BEA publishes them at the metropolitan area level, so counties are linked to their metropolitan area through the Census CBSA delineation file before joining.

The study period spans 2008 to 2023, excluding 2020. ACS suspended its 1 year estimates that year following COVID related disruptions to data collection. Because those estimates supply the occupational employment counts underlying the task group values, the task groups cannot be constructed for 2020. SAIPE publishes estimates for approximately 3,143 counties per year, giving 47,140 county year observations across the fifteen study years.1

Measuring Task Groups

Following the task framework introduced in Section 2, each of the broad occupational categories published in ACS 1 year estimates is assigned to one of four task groups: routine cognitive, routine manual, non routine cognitive, and non routine manual. The Census categories carry no such labels, so the assignment follows the classification logic of Autor and Dorn (2013): clerical and sales work as routine cognitive, production and construction work as routine manual, managerial, professional and technical work as non routine cognitive, and service work as non routine manual. For each county and year, the task group totals are the employment sums across the categories assigned to each group, shown in Equation 1.

\[ T_{g,c,t} = \sum_{o \in g} E_{o,c,t} \tag{1}\]

Here \(E_{o,c,t}\) is employment in occupational category \(o\) in county \(c\) during year \(t\), and the sum runs over the categories assigned to group \(g\). No task rating weights are applied. Each group total is a simple employment count over the categories assigned to it, so a county’s four values describe the composition of its workforce rather than a rated intensity of any task. The four totals are then divided by their sum, as in Equation 2, so that each county year is described by four values on a zero to one scale that sum to one. These four values are the county’s task score.

\[ G_{g,c,t} = \frac{T_{g,c,t}}{\sum_{g'} T_{g',c,t}} \tag{2}\]

Normalizing by the four group total converts employment counts into a composition, so counties of very different sizes are described on a common scale and the quantity of interest is the relative mix of work rather than its volume. The implications for estimation are reported in Section 4.

Retaining four separate groups, rather than collapsing them into a single routine intensity index, preserves a distinction that a composite measure erases. Under one index, a county shifting away from clerical work and a county shifting away from assembly work look identical.

Measuring Purchasing Power

The outcome variable is purchasing power, measured in dollars and defined in Equation 3 as county median household income, from the Census SAIPE program, divided by the local Regional Price Parity. BEA publishes parities as an index with the national average set to 100, so dividing by 100 converts the index into a price multiplier.

\[ A_{c,t} = \frac{\text{Median household income}_{c,t}}{\text{RPP}_{c,t} / 100} \tag{3}\]

A county with a median household income of $60,000 and a price parity of 120 has a purchasing power of $50,000, meaning local income buys what $50,000 would buy at national average prices. Stated this way, income is comparable across counties, since a given figure purchases roughly the same bundle wherever it appears.

Regional Price Parities correct for price differences between places but not between years, so purchasing power is not deflated to a constant base year and the series is in nominal dollars. Year indicators in the regression absorb whatever is common to all counties in a given year, national price movement included. The task group coefficients are therefore comparisons across counties within a year and are unaffected by this. The year effects themselves are not, since they carry price level drift alongside any other national change, and they are reported as nuisance parameters rather than as evidence of rising purchasing power.

Counties outside any metropolitan area have no published parity of their own and take their state parity instead. Within the analytical panel, 45.5 percent of county year rows use a metropolitan parity and 54.5 percent use the state fallback. This is a substantive limitation, since a single state level price index is applied to counties whose actual price levels differ, and a majority of panel rows carry it. The counties involved are above the 65,000 population threshold but predominantly outside metropolitan statistical areas, where BEA publishes no local parity.

Control Variables

Three baseline county conditions enter the models as controls. Unemployment rate comes from BLS LAUS and poverty rate from the Census SAIPE program, both measured in percentage points. Total population comes from ACS 1 year estimates and enters the models in logs. Unemployment and poverty capture labor market slack and material hardship that would otherwise be attributed to task groups. Population accounts for scale differences across counties, which range from the ACS publication threshold of 65,000 to the roughly 10.2 million residents of Los Angeles County.

Storage and Organization

Data are stored in PostgreSQL across two databases that serve different purposes.

The first is a data lake of roughly 41 tables holding raw retrievals and intermediate results, deliberately left unnormalized. Its purpose is traceability. Any figure in this report can be traced back to the records it came from, and any processing step can be rerun without returning to an agency API, including retrievals for analysis branches later scoped out.

The second is the analytical warehouse of eleven tables, whose analytical core is in third normal form. All cleaning occurs upstream, so the warehouse is effectively read only from the perspective of the analysis. Figure 1 shows the eight tables the analysis reads; a population staging table, a compatibility view, and an employment composition table retained from an earlier analysis branch are omitted.

The schema centers on a county table keyed by FIPS code, with state as a reference table. Three fact tables join to county on FIPS code and year. county_baseline holds population, median household income, poverty rate and unemployment rate, county_task_exposure holds the four task group totals from Equation 1, and county_affordability holds the outcome variable. Two table names are historical. county_task_exposure dates from an earlier measure built on O*NET task ratings that was not carried forward, and the values it holds are the employment totals of Equation 1. county_affordability and its affordability_salary column predate the purchasing power terminology used throughout this report; the quantity stored is the one defined in Equation 3. The normalized values from Equation 2 are not computed in analysis code but stored as generated columns, which the database derives from the four totals on every write. The equation is enforced in the schema itself, so no analysis can read a stale or inconsistently normalized value.

Price parities require a separate path. Metropolitan membership is kept out of the county table and in the county_cbsa_code junction table, so counties outside any metropolitan area carry no null CBSA fields, and the crosswalk stands alone as its own fact. From there cbsa connects to cbsa_rpp. Storing parities at the CBSA level reflects the level at which BEA measures them and avoids repeating a single value across every county in a metropolitan area.

The Analytical Dataset

The 47,140 county year observations described above are the records the source data support, not the records the models estimate on. Two filters reduce it.

Task group coverage is the binding constraint. The occupational employment counts that build the four task groups come from ACS 1 year estimates, which the Census Bureau publishes only for areas with populations above 65,000. Counties below that threshold have no occupational estimates in any year, so no task groups can be constructed for them. This leaves 12,087 county year observations across 848 counties, roughly a quarter of the 47,140.

Requiring non missing controls removes a further 104 observations. Every one of the 104 is missing an unemployment rate, giving BLS LAUS a coverage rate of 99.1 percent across the retained counties. Population, median household income and poverty rate are complete. The estimation sample is therefore 11,983 county year observations, and all models reported in Section 4 and Section 5 fit on these same rows.

The 848 counties are also the unit the machine learning models split on. Because a county appears in the panel up to fifteen times, splitting rows at random would place the same county in both training and test data, so the split is grouped by county and every observation of a given county falls entirely on one side. This makes 848, rather than 11,983, the effective sample size for held out evaluation. The reasoning behind the grouped split is taken up in Section 4.

The population threshold shapes what the results can describe. The 848 retained counties hold the large majority of the United States population but a minority of its counties, and they are more metropolitan, more populous and more economically diverse than those dropped. Rural counties are underrepresented as a direct consequence. The restriction covers the regression coefficients and the machine learning results alike, so the partial dependence curves and importance rankings in Section 5 describe how the models behave across counties above 65,000 residents rather than across counties in general. Section 6 returns to this limitation and to one approach for addressing it in future work.

Analysis

Panel Regression

The four task group values described in Equation 2 sum to one, so all four cannot enter a regression together. One serves as the reference group, and we use non routine cognitive. Each remaining coefficient represents the change in purchasing power associated with a shift out of non routine cognitive work and into that group, holding the controls fixed. Each coefficient is a comparison against the reference group. The group values are measured on a zero to one scale. Per percentage point figures reported later therefore divide the estimated coefficients by 100.

Year indicators capture conditions common to all counties within a year. These include the 2008 recession, the recovery through the 2010s, and the price movement that purchasing power does not itself account for. Because purchasing power is not deflated to a constant base year, the year effects carry price level drift alongside any other national change and are treated as nuisance parameters rather than as evidence of rising purchasing power. The task group coefficients are comparisons across counties within a year and are unaffected by this.

Standard errors are clustered by county, because repeated observations of the same county across fifteen years are not independent draws. A Durbin Watson statistic of 0.496 confirms that dependence directly. The estimated coefficients are reported in Section 5.

Collinearity with Reference Group

Normalizing the four totals by their sum, as Section 3 describes, also resolves a collinearity problem that would otherwise make these coefficients unusable. A variance inflation factor measures how much a coefficient is destabilized by its correlation with the other inputs, with a value above 10 the conventional threshold for concern. The unnormalized totals in Equation 1 carry factors between 13 and 27, because all four are employment counts that scale together with county size, so they move together and carry little independent information. Dividing by the four group total removes that shared scale. Figure 2 shows the two specifications side by side: on the specification actually estimated, every factor sits between 1.2 and 1.6.

This also explains a problem visible earlier in the project. Before normalization the coefficients were unstable and their standard errors were large relative to their magnitudes, both standard symptoms of multicollinearity.

Data validation

Before making any estimations, we ensure that the data support the chosen methods. We examine the distributions of the four task groups and purchasing power to identify any skewness or outliers. Additionally, we verify that the county year coverage is consistent across the retained panel. Furthermore, we check for any overlap between task groups to ensure that no two groups effectively carry the same information. Figure 3 provides visual representations of the distributions.

Purchasing power is right skewed, with a skewness of 1.17. The center of the distribution is located near $57,000, and a long tail of high purchasing power counties extends above it. This skewness is why the diagnostics below focus on the extremes of the distribution.

Specification Diagnostics

A linear model assumes a constant rate of association across the data range and symmetrical, evenly distributed errors around the fit. We verify these assumptions using three diagnostics: residuals plotted against fitted values, a QQ plot of the residuals, and the variance inflation factors reported above. Figure 4 illustrates the first two, pinpointing the failure directly. The smoothed residual line deviates from zero, indicating curvature the model does not capture. The vertical spread of residuals increases with fitted values. The model underestimates both the lowest and highest purchasing power counties while fitting the middle well. Additionally, the QQ plot deviates from normality in the upper tail, reflecting a residual skew of 1.02.

The level model still provides the interpretable estimates we report. Additionally, its own diagnostics demonstrate that the data possess a structure that a straight line in dollars cannot adequately represent.

Logarithmic Respecification

Underprediction at both extremes is a hallmark of a multiplicative process that is fitted on an additive scale. The factors associated with purchasing power operate proportionally, so the same shift in an input moves a high purchasing power county by more dollars than a low one. Logging the outcome converts those proportional effects into the additive form a linear model assumes. Figure 5 shows the outcome itself before and after the transformation.

Refitting the same specification on log purchasing power resolves most of the failure, as Figure 6 demonstrates. The residual skew decreases from 1.02 to 0.09, and the spread becomes relatively constant across the fitted range. The QQ plot also straightens through most of its range. The log linear fit serves as the baseline for evaluating the flexible models presented in the subsequent section.

Figure 7 puts the same comparison in the units of the outcome. Fitted values are cut into twenty equal count bins, and each point compares a bin’s mean prediction with its mean actual value, so points on the line indicate no systematic bias in that range. On the level scale the model tracks purchasing power closely through the middle of the distribution, where most counties sit, but both tail bins come in roughly $8,000 to $9,000 above their predictions. On the log scale the points track the line throughout.

The smoothed residual line does not lie perfectly flat even after the transformation. The remaining deviations are structured in a way that a single global transformation cannot absorb, most likely due to interactions among the inputs. These deviations motivate the increase in model complexity.

Variance Decomposition

Purchasing power and the task groups vary both across counties and within a county over time, and the two carry different implications. Figure 8 splits each group’s variation into the two components. The manual groups vary almost entirely between counties, which is what makes the associated purchasing power differences durable rather than temporary. Routine cognitive is the only group with genuine within county movement.

The Census occupation coding change between 2009 and 2010, as described in Section 3, inflates apparent within county variation for the routine cognitive group. This is due to the way the source data were coded, not a property of the counties, so the figure above is computed on the panel restricted to 2010 onward with each year’s cross county mean removed. We additionally repeat the main estimates on pre pandemic (2010 to 2019) and post pandemic (2021 to 2023) windows. Coefficient stability across these windows is reported in Section 5.

Predictive Modeling

Beyond the inferential panel regression above, we evaluate three predictive approaches of increasing complexity: linear regression, random forest, and a small neural network. We check whether a flexible model captures meaningfully more signal than the linear specification. We reuse the county year panel already assembled above, extended with state identifiers for the fixed effects comparison below.

Feature Set and Reference Groups

The task groups are computed directly by the database as each group’s count divided by the four group sum, so they already satisfy the constraint used in the panel regression above and require no additional normalization here. As before, one group must be dropped to avoid a deterministic column, and we retain non_routine_cognitive_share as the omitted reference for consistency with the panel regression.

Collinearity

Our four main features, routine_cognitive_share, routine_manual_share, non_routine_cognitive_share and non_routine_manual_share, are parts of a whole, so one must be removed to maintain independence, as the fourth is deterministic once the values of the other three are known. Leaving all four in would guarantee at least one variance inflation offender, for the same reason set out in Figure 2 above.

The remaining three coefficients read as the change in purchasing power associated with a shift toward that group and away from the omitted one.

Grouped Evaluation Design

This is not a forecasting exercise, so a time ordered split is not required for its usual reason. A grouped split is still necessary because a county’s economic profile barely moves year to year, so its 2018 and 2019 rows are near duplicates. If two adjacent rows land on opposite sides of the split, the model can recall a county rather than learn a relationship. We therefore group by county_fips when splitting, so every test set prediction comes from a county the model has seen no rows of. This tests whether the pattern is relationship driven rather than memorized.

We report two versions of each linear model throughout this section: a baseline, using the task groups with poverty and unemployment only, and a full model, which adds year and state controls. The baseline shows what the relationship looks like before time and geography are accounted for, and the full model is the one carried forward as the primary result. Reporting both makes visible exactly what changes when time and geography enter, and why.

Neural Network

The neural network is kept shallow and narrow. The outcome is continuous, so the network ends in a single linear output unit.

The initial training curve shows training loss decreasing steadily and plateauing below 0.1, while validation loss initially decreases to approximately 0.2 before rising to roughly 0.5, a clear indication of overfitting. We address this with L2 weight regularization, increased dropout, and a shorter early stopping patience.

After these changes the gap between training and validation loss narrows. Additional tuning could likely narrow it further.

The current results are sufficient, however, to conclude that the neural network does not outperform the random forest or the log target linear model, both of which are substantially cheaper to fit and tune. Given this project’s emphasis on explaining the relationship between task groups and purchasing power rather than maximizing predictive accuracy, we do not pursue further tuning of the neural network and proceed with the linear and random forest models as the primary results.

Summary

Three specifications answer three different questions. The panel regression, with county clustered standard errors, is the model used for inference. It shows that routine intensive work is associated with lower purchasing power even after controlling for poverty, unemployment, time, and geography, and the variance decomposition establishes that the task group differences behind that association are durable features of a place rather than transient ones. Its log respecification resolves the residual violations that make the raw target version untrustworthy for that purpose.

The predictive models answer the different question of how much of purchasing power can be explained at all, and by what. The random forest and the log target linear model perform comparably on held out data, which indicates that the curvature in the relationship adds little once poverty, unemployment, time, and geography are already in the model. The neural network does not improve on either, and its added complexity is not justified by this feature set.

Across both approaches, the task groups carry real, independent explanatory power, but poverty rate remains the dominant single factor throughout. This ordering, not any one model’s fit statistic, is the finding this analysis is built to support, and it is what Section 5 carries forward.

Results

Four findings organize this section. A one percentage point shift toward non routine manual work, away from non routine cognitive, is associated with roughly a thousand dollars less in purchasing power, and that association survives poverty, unemployment, and the year and state controls. The relationship is proportional rather than fixed in dollars, so a flexible model cannot improve on a linear one once the outcome is logged. Poverty rate carries the most explanatory weight in every model, with the task groups adding to it rather than substituting for it. And because a county’s task groups barely move across fifteen years, the differences they are associated with are durable features of place.

Where purchasing power is strained

Purchasing power has clear geographic structure before any modeling. The counties where income stretches furthest are not the counties where income is highest, which is the gap the price adjustment is built to close.

Task groups and purchasing power

The panel regression returns a negative association between routine intensive work and purchasing power that holds after controls. A one percentage point shift toward non routine manual work is associated with roughly $846 (± $38) less in purchasing power, evaluated at the panel mean. Figure 15 reports all three coefficients against the non routine cognitive reference.

The associations survive the addition of poverty rate and the other baseline controls, so the task groups are not standing in for poverty. Figure 16 puts the coefficients before and after controls side by side. The task group coefficients hold their direction and relative ordering while poverty rate grows in magnitude. Poverty is the larger coefficient of the two, but it does not absorb the task group association: the controls sharpen that association rather than displacing it.

What a straight line misses

The relationship between a task group and purchasing power does not hold at a constant rate across its range. Figure 17 bins counties by routine cognitive employment and plots the actual mean against the random forest prediction in each bin. The two track each other closely, and the shape they trace is not a line.

That curvature is what motivates the flexible models, but it turns out to be worth little once the outcome is placed on the right scale. What the models weigh

Poverty rate carries the largest importance value in the random forest by a wide margin, as Figure 19 shows. The task groups follow, with non routine manual highest among them. This ordering is consistent with the regression: poverty carries most of the explanatory weight, and the task groups add to it rather than substituting for it.

Importance measures how much prediction degrades when a feature is scrambled, so it is a statement about the model rather than about the world. Read that way, the ordering says the model leans hardest on poverty rate and that the task groups are the next thing it will not give up. The ablations in Section 4 say the same thing from the other direction: removing the task groups costs the model real accuracy, and the task groups alone recover most but not all of the full model’s fit. Among the task groups, non routine manual is the one the model is most sensitive to, matching its position as the steepest coefficient in Figure 15.

Figure 20 asks a different question: not how much each task group matters, but what shape the relationship takes. Each curve traces predicted purchasing power across the observed range of one group, holding the other features at their observed distribution. The curves are not flat, which is why the groups carry importance, and they are not straight, which is why the linear model needed the log scale.

Stability over time

A county’s task groups move very little across the fifteen year panel. Table 6 reports the rank correlation between each county’s first and last observed year for all four groups. Counties largely hold their relative position throughout.

This matters for interpretation. Because a county’s task groups are close to a fixed characteristic of place, the purchasing power differences associated with them do not fade, and they are not something residents can be expected to move out from under. Figure 21 shows the same point in aggregate: the composition shifts gradually rather than abruptly.

Robustness

Restricting the panel to 2010 onward, which removes the occupation coding change described in Section 3, leaves the main associations intact. Splitting further into pre pandemic and post pandemic windows gives the same picture: non routine manual is stable within 2.6 percent across eras, routine manual within 6.6 percent, routine cognitive within 12.5 percent, and the ordering of the three is identical in every window.

Conclusions

Summary of Findings

We asked whether counties with more routine work offer less purchasing power to the people who live in them. Building a county by year panel from five federal sources, summarizing each county’s work by four task groups, and measuring purchasing power in dollars as median household income adjusted for local prices, we find that the answer is yes and that the relationship holds once poverty and unemployment are accounted for. Poverty is by a wide margin the strongest single correlate of county purchasing power. The task groups are a smaller but separate one.

Contributions

Two patterns are worth stating plainly before the broader claim. Poverty is the strongest single correlate of county purchasing power, but the task groups relate to purchasing power independently of it. And neither relationship is a straight line: the binned means, the partial dependence curves, and the fact that a linear model matches the random forest only after the outcome is logged all point to a proportional rather than fixed dollar relationship.

The task framework has been applied to wages and to employment composition, mostly at the national or commuting zone level. Applying it to a cost adjusted outcome at the county level changes what the measure describes: not what work pays, but what that pay buys where it is earned. Two findings follow from that shift. The association is present after controlling for local economic distress, so it is not poverty in different clothing. And it is not linear, which the diagnostics establish and the flexible models exploit, so studies that assume a straight line relationship between task groups and local outcomes are leaving structure on the table.

The finding lands on people through place. Residents of routine work counties see their incomes buy systematically less, and because a county’s task groups barely move across fifteen years, the difference is built into where they live rather than something individual effort resolves. Three groups can act on that. Economic development agencies deciding where workforce training goes, since the task groups separate structurally exposed counties from those in a temporary downturn. Policymakers and planners designing cost of living responses, since these gaps will not close on their own. And researchers extending the framework, since both the county level link and its nonlinear shape point past existing national wage studies.

Ethical Considerations

All data are public county level aggregates with no individual records, so no person can be identified, and no individual level inference is licensed. Reading a county level association at the level of a person is the ecological fallacy: nothing here says that any particular worker in a routine work county has lower purchasing power, only that counties with more routine work tend to have less of it.

The panel also carries an equity constraint. The population threshold below which occupational estimates are not published removes small counties entirely, so rural places are least represented in exactly the kind of analysis that might direct resources toward them.

Two harms are worth naming. Labeling counties as exposed or stuck could stigmatize them or steer investment away from the places that need it. And reading the association as causal could motivate policy that tries to reshape a county’s mix of work in the expectation that purchasing power follows, which this study does not support.

Limitations

The results are associational. Counties are not assigned their mix of work, and nothing in this design isolates a causal channel, so we cannot say that routine work makes a place offer less purchasing power, only that the two move together.

The four task groups are parts of a whole and sum to one, so one had to serve as the reference group. Every coefficient is read relative to non routine cognitive work rather than in isolation.

Coverage is the sharpest constraint. Task group values exist for 12,087 of the 47,140 county year observations in the frame, and dropping the 104 rows missing an unemployment rate leaves 11,983 for estimation across 848 counties. The counties that fall out are small ones, below the population threshold at which the American Community Survey publishes one year occupational estimates, so the panel describes metropolitan and larger non metropolitan counties rather than the country.

Regional Price Parities are published for metropolitan areas, so counties outside one take their state value. Roughly 54.5 percent of county year rows use that fallback, which applies a single price index to counties whose actual price levels differ, and it falls disproportionately on rural places.

2020 is excluded because the American Community Survey did not release one year estimates for it. The 2009 to 2010 occupation coding change is a source artifact that inflates measured within county variance for routine cognitive work; restricting to 2010 onward leaves the conclusions unchanged.

Future Directions

Two extensions would address the limitations above directly.

The coverage gap could be narrowed with satellite imagery. Jean et al. (2016) estimate local economic conditions from daytime and nighttime imagery where survey data are thin, an approach that could recover the small counties this panel excludes and allow the purchasing power pattern to be retested on them.

Finer grained labor data would move the work closer to a causal claim. Splitting the task groups by worker education and status would separate the effect of workforce composition from the effect of the task groups themselves, which the current design cannot disentangle.

Conclusion

Poverty remains the strongest correlate of county purchasing power, and nothing in this analysis displaces it. What the analysis adds is that the mix of work a county holds is a separate pressure on top of it, one that poverty and unemployment statistics alone do not capture. A county can look unremarkable on every conventional distress measure and still hold a workforce whose dollars systematically buy less, year after year, because of what its residents do for a living. That pressure is durable, since task groups are close to a fixed characteristic of place, and the people living with it cannot be expected to out earn a structure built into where they live. Anyone deciding where help should go, and reading only the poverty rate to decide it, is looking at an incomplete picture.
